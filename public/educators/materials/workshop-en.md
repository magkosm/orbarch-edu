# The Architect's Mind in Orbit — Workshop Plan (EN)

**Audience:** ages 12–18 (adapt depth by age band — 12–13 · 14–15 · 16–18; core pilot: Grade 8/9 / ~14) ·
**Duration:** \~2 hours — the blocks as written add up to **\~105–130 minutes**; a **\~90–100 minute** version
is possible by cutting the Model Lab and the A3 group sketch and shortening A2/A5 (see *Running long?* under
[Differentiation & tips](#differentiation--tips)) ·
**Group format:** run sequentially for multiple classes (pilot: 3 × ~20 students).
**Theme:** Space-station architecture and how it shapes crew cognitive performance.

**Devices:** every student uses their **own mobile phone**. The teacher has a
**projector** and **whiteboard**. All tools are mobile-friendly at
**https://magkosm.github.io/orbarch-edu**.

> Language: English plan. Same structure in [Swedish](./workshop-sv.md) and
> [Greek](./workshop-el.md). Links use `?lng=en` — swap to `?lng=sv` / `?lng=el`.

---

## Learning objectives

By the end, students can:
1. Name environmental factors that raise or lower stress in space **and** in everyday places.
2. Explain how scientists **measure** cognition (reaction time, working memory, executive function)
   and **compare conditions** (baseline vs. stressor), including learning effects and normalization.
3. Apply design **trade-offs** in the Blueprint Designer: crew cognition vs. mission engineering/science.

## Block A — Presentation & whiteboard discussion (~35–45 min)

### A1 · Opening (5 min)

1. **Introduce the speaker.**
2. **Slide 1** — title slide from the pitch deck (*The Architect's Mind in Orbit*).

### A2 · What is a space station? History (~8 min)

- **Definition:** a spacecraft that stays in orbit so crews can **live and work** there for weeks to months — not a brief visit. (The first station crew, on Salyut 1 in 1971, stayed 24 days; ISS crews stay about 6 months.)
- **History arc** (name each and **where it was built**):
  - **Salyut** (USSR) · **Skylab** (USA) · **Mir** (USSR/Russia) · **ISS** (international) · **Tiangong** (China)
- **Design thread:** stations were often **messy and machine-centric** — equipment first, humans adapting later.

> **English / international cohort:** mention ESA membership and astronaut participation on the ISS where relevant.

### A3 · State of the art — messy reality (~5 min)

- Show the **ISS interior photo Marcus Wandt took in the Destiny lab** (or the Axiom Space photo of Wandt in ESA's Columbus lab): cables everywhere, cramped, utilitarian. (Image credits: *ESA – M. Wandt* / *Axiom Space – M. López-Alegría*; links under [References](#references--further-reading).)
- Land the point: this is **almost state of the art today** — messy, cables, **loud**, poor lighting, cramped, complex and convoluted.
- **Same photo + text box:** *"What makes space stressful?"*

### A4 · Student discussion on the whiteboard (~10 min)

Open the floor. **Prompt in three passes** (capture everything on the board — it stays up all class):

1. **Space:** what would stress you in orbit?
2. **School / classroom / home:** same questions — transfer the idea.
3. **Recovery:** what helps you **relax** in your environment?

**Seed ideas if needed:** adaptation sickness · new frame of reference · lack of gravity · atmosphere change · schedule · risk · affordances · diet · food/water quality and quantity · exercise · muscle and blood issues · windows · natural elements · privacy · clear understanding of the layout · noise · clutter · light · daylight · circadian rhythm · and more.

**Guide toward three cognitive-link elements** through questions (attention, stress/recovery, performance), then show:

> `[SLIDE — The cognitive link]` (biophilia, daylight, acoustics, order → calmer, sharper brain)

### A5 · Why we care & the long view (~8 min)

1. **Why measure this?** Better stations need evidence, not guesswork.
2. **Current stations slide** — what we have now (tie back to the photo).
3. **Future vision slide** — NASA Ames studies, **Rick Guidice** artwork (rotating habitats, greenery, windows).
4. **Message:** reaching that — perhaps centuries away (the speaker's own estimate, not a forecast) — takes **small steps today**. Small **quantitative** experiments → **qualitative** differences later. *We are taking those small steps now.*

### A6 · How we test cognition (~8 min)

**School analogy:** *How does your math teacher know if you're good at math?* → **tests**.

**Three research tests** (one sentence each):

| Test | Measures | Workshop use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PVT / Reaction Time** | Alertness & speed | All ages — warm-up today |
| **N-Back** | Working memory | Mention; use with **ages 16–18** |
| **MATB (2-min)** | Executive function / multitasking | Focus today (**ages 14–18**) |

> Younger classes: *"We'll look deeper another time"* — RT for ages 12–13, MATB for 14–15, N-Back for 16–18.

**Where this research has run:**

- **MDRS** (Mars Desert Research Station) with **ISAE-SUPAERO**
- **ISS** with **Marcus Wandt** (2024)

## Block B — Interactive experiments (~45–50 min)

Use **handout section 1** throughout this block.

### B1 · Reaction Time — one round (~8 min)

1. Show **QR for Reaction Time only**. Screen: *Reaction Time Test* → **Start Test**. A red dot appears **8 times** at random gaps (1.5–8 s) — **tap the dot** as fast as you can (the on-screen text says SPACE; on a phone it's a tap). Tapping early is a "false alarm" and delays the dot. ~1 minute. Results page: **Average Reaction Time**, fastest, slowest — write the **average** on the handout. Ignore the save/name button.
2. Gather a few scores aloud — expect **wide spread**.
3. **Why so different?** Age, gaming, sleep, breakfast, mood, phone lag, how many times you've done this before… These are **confounders** — things that change the score but are not the thing we're testing.
4. **So we don't compare you with your neighbour.** We compare **you with you**: same person, same phone, same day — only the room changes. And we express the change as a **percentage of your own score**, so a 9,000 → 8,100 student and a 6,000 → 5,400 student have had the *same* −10 % effect. That "divide by your own baseline" step is what scientists call **normalizing**. (Board: write the two examples.)

### B2 · MATB taught on the projector (~10 min)

**Do not hand out the MATB QR yet.**

1. On the **projector** open the hub `/` → tap **Presets** → the modal lists *Quick Test (2 min)* and, under **Training Modes**, *Communications Training · System Monitoring Training · Tracking Training · Resource Management Training* (direct links: `/comms` `/monitoring` `/tracking` `/resource`). Use a browser zoom of ~80 % so all four panels fit on the screen.
2. **Full MATB first** (Quick Test 2 min): dismiss the overlay, let it run ~30 s. *"Four jobs at once. The SYSTEM STATUS panel shows Health (keep it high) and Load. Your score is Health added up every second — max 12,000 for 2 minutes."* Tap **Quit Game**.
3. **One task at a time** (~2 min each). Each Training page opens with that task's instruction box — read it aloud in your own words, tap *I understand — Start*, show 3–4 events, then **Quit Game**. Say what to do **on a phone**:
   - **System Monitoring:** *"Tap F1 when it stops being green, F2 when it goes red, F3–F6 when the dot leaves the grey centre."*
   - **Tracking:** *"When AUTO turns to MANUAL, drag the cursor back into the small square with your finger."*
   - **Communications:** *"Listen. If the message says your callsign (shown at the top), tick that radio and tap − / + (−− / ++ for big jumps) until the frequency matches. If it's another callsign — ignore it."*
   - **Resource Management:** *"Keep tanks A and B near the red line by tapping pumps on/off. A red pump has failed — use another."*
4. Back to the full MATB for 20 s: *"Now all four together. Which one will you drop first?"* — that's the executive-function question.

### B3 · MATB dry run (~8 min)

1. Release **MATB 2-min QR**.
2. Everyone runs a **practice (dry) run** — walk the room; make sure students understand all four tasks.
3. **What students see:** a dark instruction box titled *Multitasking* → tap **"I understand — Start"** . Top bar: **Time** counting down from 02:00, **Score**, red **Quit Game**. Tell them: *"Don't quit — let the 2 minutes run out."*
4. **At the end** the screen says **Simulation Complete** and, at the top, **Final Score: N** — that is the number for the handout. Ignore "Export data (CSV)", "Export raw data" and "Export plot data" (researcher tools). If a **"New High Score!"** box asks for a name → tap **Skip**. Never type a real name. Then **Return to Main Menu** (or just switch tab, see B4).
5. Write the Final Score in the **Practice** row of the handout — you'll need it in B5.

### B4 · Condition Lab experiment (~12–14 min)

1. **Explain (1 min):** *"Same 2-minute test, twice. ① in quiet, ② with cabin noise. We compare each person with themselves."*
2. Show **Condition Lab QR**. Students see: a **Cabin noise stressor** button (*Noise OFF — tap to play*), a **Volume** slider, and two cards — **① Baseline (quiet)** with *Run MATB (quiet)* and **② Stressed (noise)** with *Run MATB (with noise)*. Each card has an **Enter final score** box.
3. **Run ① as a class:** speakers **off**, room quiet. On your count everyone taps **Run MATB (quiet)** → the MATB opens in a **new tab** → *I understand — Start* → 2 min. Tell them beforehand: *"When it ends, read Final Score, then come back to the Condition Lab tab (tap the tabs icon in your browser) and type it under ①."*
4. **Run ② as a class:** start the **room speakers** (cabin noise at a level where you still have to raise your voice slightly). Everyone taps **Run MATB (with noise)** — this also switches the phone's own noise **on**; ask them to keep phone volume at the default (about half). Same routine; type the score under **②**.
5. When both boxes are filled the app shows a **Comparison** bar chart and one sentence: *"Performance dropped X% under noise."* or *"Performance held up or improved (+X%) under noise."* Copy the X and the direction to the handout. (X = (② − ①) ÷ ① × 100 — say this out loud for the older students.)
6. **Expect mixed results — that's the next discussion (B5), not a failure.**

> **If the noise stops on some phones** when the MATB tab opens (iPhones may pause audio in background tabs), that's fine — the room speakers are the stressor; the phone noise is a backup for students with headphones.

> **Noise level:** cabin noise should be *annoying, not painful* — you can still be understood if you raise your voice. Keep in-app volume at the default; earphone users must not max it out.

### B5 · Learning effects & the science (~8 min)

On the **whiteboard**:

1. **Learning effect:** ask a student who plays sports for years — *what happens when you start a **new** sport?* Session-to-session improvement → sketch a **rising curve**.
2. *Does it rise forever?* → **No** → **performance plateau**, then smaller swings from mood, sleep, food.
3. Analogy: well-fed, rested pro athlete vs. stressed, tired, hungry — same person, different day.
4. **Look at your own rows:** *Practice → ① — both quiet. Did ① beat Practice? That gap is the learning effect — you just measured it.*
5. **The flaw in today's experiment:** run ② is always second, so practice pushes it **up** while noise pushes it **down** — they can cancel. That is why the class is mixed. Real studies (a) repeat the quiet run until scores **stabilize**, and (b) **counterbalance** — half the participants get noise first. Today was a taste of that protocol.
6. **Class tally (2 min):** three columns on the board — ↓ (worse by >5 %) · ≈ (within ±5 %) · ↑ (better by >5 %). Hands up per column. *"Which column would you bet on if we did this 100 times?"*
7. **Advanced teachers:** plot class scores on the board to show trends.

Students complete **handout §1** (scores, % change, short reflection).

## Block C — Design studio (~20–30 min)

### C1 · Space Architecture Simulator (~5 min)

- Students open **Simulator QR**: four sliders (Noise · Biophilia · Clutter · Lighting) and four bars (Attention · Working Memory · Stress · Overall). Task, 3 min: *"Push Noise to 100 — what happens to Overall? Reset. Now find the single slider that moves Overall the most."* (Default Overall ≈ 61; Noise 100 → ≈ 40.)
- Land it: *"This is an illustrative model inspired by research — many small experiments like yours. It is not clinical data (it says so at the bottom)."* The studies behind the idea are listed under [References](#references--further-reading).

### C2 · Habitat Blueprint Designer (~15 min)

1. Explain the **mission brief**: some features help **crew cognition**, others help **engineering or science** — real trade-offs.
2. **Phone gestures:** tap a feature chip → read its one-line effect in the box below → tap grid cells to place; **Erase** chip to remove; **Load scenario** restores the start, **Clear module** empties the grid. The **🚪 entrance** is the middle cell of the left column.
3. **Corridor rule:** every module must touch an empty cell that connects, through empty cells, back to 🚪. A module with a **red border** is cut off — repeat this rule at least three times (pilot lesson). Say: *"Leave a hallway."*
4. **The starting scenario scores 45.** Ticking the checklist alone gets you to ~50; to reach **70** you must *also* quiet and green the module (acoustic zones, plant wall, circadian light) while keeping enough science and life support. That tension is the lesson.
5. Students complete **handout §2** and write their **Simulated reaction time** next to the score — *"the same number you measured on yourself in B1, now predicted from your design."*
6. Find a student at ≥ 70 → **rebuild their grid on the teacher laptop** (7×5, about a minute; ask them to read out row by row) or mirror the phone, and project it. Debrief questions: *What did you remove and what did it cost (Science / Life support)? Which single feature moved Mission Success the most? Would the crew agree with your trade?*
7. **2-minute warning** before you stop — the pilot found this activity "addictive". Fast finishers: **Mission Success ≥ 78**.

### C3 · Optional — Model Lab (~10 min, if time)

- Show **Model Lab QR** (or do it on the projector only). Steps: **+ Add slider** → name it from the whiteboard list (e.g. *Crowding*, 0–100) → on an outcome tap **weights** → set the new slider's weight (negative = harmful, positive = helpful) → **done**. **Show interaction map** to see the wiring (solid = input → outcome, dashed = input → input, green good / red bad).
- Discuss: *"Who decides the weight? What experiment would tell us?"*
- The model is saved **on that device** — tap **Reset to example** before the next class.
- **Trim this first** if running long.

### C4 · Optional — A3 group sketch (if time)

In groups: design the **interior of a space station** on A3 (use handout §3 or extra paper). Label features tied to the whiteboard.

## Data, names & privacy

- **Nothing leaves the phone.** All tools run in the browser; scores and any CSV export stay on the device. There is no account and no upload.
- **No real names in the app.** If a "New High Score" box appears, tap **Skip**.
- **Sticky notes:** the name is for you (to see who wrote what). For a wall display, fold the name under or copy the ideas onto a fresh sheet.
- **Handout scores:** keep the sheets in class. If you want a class dataset (great for a maths lesson: mean % change, spread), copy scores with first names only or numbers.
- Do not send student data to the researchers; the workshop is a teaching activity, not a study. Questions → ESERO contact on the last slide.

## Differentiation & tips

- **Ages 12–13:** emphasize Reaction Time; mention MATB lightly; skip N-Back detail.
- **Ages 14–15 (core pilot, ~14):** full Block B as written — MATB is the core test.
- **Ages 16–18:** add N-Back demo; deeper normalization and Model Lab.
- **No phone / dead battery / no Wi-Fi:** pair up (pilot-tested — one device per pair works; the partner reads out events). Bring 2–3 spare tablets or laptops; every tool also runs on the teacher laptop.
- **Hearing impairment / no earphones:** the Communications task is audio-only. That student runs the other three tasks and ignores Comms (Health drops a bit — fine), or pairs with a partner who relays the radio calls. Their **% change** is still valid because both runs have the same handicap.
- **Sensory sensitivity:** keep phone noise at the default (half) volume; anyone may opt out of run ② and use the class tally instead.
- **Vision / colour:** dark UI, small text — allow browser zoom, phones in landscape for the MATB; Monitoring uses green/red on F1/F2 — say *"stopped being green / turned red"* and point at the shape, not the colour.
- **Language:** append `?lng=sv` / `?lng=el` — the comms audio is recorded in all three languages. The MATB instruction boxes are dense; read them aloud in your own words (see B2), don't make students read them on a phone.
- **Motor / fast tapping:** Tracking in touch mode is drag-based; a stylus or a laptop with keys (WASD) is an easy accommodation.
- **MATB uneven?** Demo on projector first; pair struggling students; near-perfect scores and strugglers can coexist.
- **Blueprint corridor rule:** repeat entrance-path rule — see [workshop-feedback.md](./workshop-feedback.md).
- **Safari quirks?** Prefer Chrome on student phones.
- **Running long?** Cut Model Lab → cut A3 group sketch → shorten history (keep whiteboard discussion).
- **QR discipline:** one QR on screen at a time reduces confusion (pilot-tested).
